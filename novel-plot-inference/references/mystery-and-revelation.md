# Mystery and Revelation

## Purpose

Control what characters and readers know, when they know it, and what evidence justifies a conclusion.

## 1. Keep knowledge channels separate

Track independently:
- objective story facts;
- each character's knowledge;
- each character's belief and suspicion;
- what each character has been told;
- what the reader has observed;
- what the author has not decided;
- what is deliberately withheld.

Never give a character information merely because it is present in the author's notes.

## 2. Clue design

For each clue, identify:
- observable form;
- who can encounter it;
- what it directly establishes;
- what it may suggest;
- what it does not establish;
- possible alternative explanations;
- when it is introduced;
- what later event or evidence may clarify it.

A clue should not prove more than its content supports. Similarity, repetition, resemblance, and thematic echo are not automatically proof of identity or causation.

## 3. Suspicion versus confirmation

Use precise stages:
- noticed;
- questioned;
- interpreted;
- suspected;
- tested;
- supported;
- confirmed.

These are not mandatory story beats. They prevent an unsupported jump from a vague clue to certainty.

## 4. Fair revelation

A revelation should:
- answer a question the story has actually raised;
- be supported by previously available evidence or an explicitly introduced new source;
- explain why the character can now understand it;
- preserve any remaining uncertainty that the author intends to keep;
- change the character's next decision or the reader's interpretation.

Do not retroactively claim that an earlier clue meant something that it could not reasonably support.

## 5. Misleading information

A misleading explanation may come from:
- incomplete evidence;
- a mistaken character;
- a source with limited knowledge;
- deliberate deception, only if deception is established;
- a correct observation interpreted incorrectly.

Do not invent a liar, conspiracy, hidden observer, or deceptive motive merely to create a twist.

## 6. Reveal order

When planning reveals, compare:
- what the reader knows before the character;
- what the character knows before the reader;
- whether the asymmetry is intentional;
- what action becomes possible after the reveal;
- which questions remain open;
- whether the reveal violates any project timing restriction.

Do not reveal an identity, relationship, mechanism, or final answer earlier than the project permits.

## 7. Mystery audit

- [ ] The question is legible.
- [ ] The clue actually exists before the reveal.
- [ ] The clue's evidentiary strength is not overstated.
- [ ] Character knowledge is chronologically possible.
- [ ] Suspicion is not presented as confirmation.
- [ ] The reveal changes the story state.
- [ ] Remaining unknowns remain unknown.
