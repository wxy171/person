# Plot Architecture

## Purpose

Help design story structure at the scale requested by the author without imposing a universal template, act count, chapter count, or ending.

## 1. Choose the working scale

Possible scales include:
- beat: one meaningful change or decision;
- scene: one continuous dramatic unit;
- sequence: connected scenes pursuing a local objective;
- arc: a character or conflict progression across multiple sequences;
- volume: a larger unit defined by the project;
- mainline: the overall direction of the story.

Do not decide the project's volume count, chapter count, or boundaries unless the author has authorized it.

## 2. Work outward from the current story state

For a larger structure, identify:
- current protagonist goal;
- immediate unresolved problem;
- established long-term direction, if any;
- active character arcs;
- existing mysteries and reveal restrictions;
- confirmed turning points;
- unresolved structural decisions.

Then propose what the next unit needs to accomplish. Do not force every plotline to advance in every unit.

## 3. Structure as state change

A useful unit-level question is:
- What is true at the beginning?
- What does the focal character try to change?
- What resists or complicates the attempt?
- What choice or event changes the situation?
- What is true at the end that was not true before?
- What new action becomes necessary?

This is a reasoning scaffold, not a fixed beat sheet.

## 4. Interweave plotlines

When multiple plotlines are active, distinguish:
- shared event;
- shared information;
- shared character;
- shared consequence;
- thematic resemblance only.

Do not merge two plotlines merely because their themes or symbols resemble each other. Their causal connection must be established or clearly proposed.

## 5. Pacing

Pacing depends on the density and placement of meaningful changes, not only chapter length.

Check:
- how long the character pursues one objective without new information or changed tactics;
- whether exposition delays an action the character could take;
- whether a reveal arrives before the story has raised the question;
- whether consequences are given enough space to register;
- whether repeated scenes change the situation;
- whether quiet scenes deepen a relationship, clarify a choice, or preserve meaningful tension.

Do not apply fixed word counts or chapter quotas unless the project requires them.

## 6. Endings and long-range planning

If the author has not decided the ending, keep it unresolved. You may compare possible directions only when asked, and label them as proposals.

Do not assume every story must resolve every mystery, restore an earlier state, defeat an antagonist, or end with a final confrontation.

## 7. Structure audit

A structural proposal should identify:
- the purpose of each unit;
- the character goal driving it;
- the key decision or state change;
- the causal bridge to the next unit;
- which plotlines advance or deliberately pause;
- dependencies on unresolved author decisions.
